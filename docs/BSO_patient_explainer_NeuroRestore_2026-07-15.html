BRAIN·SPINE ORCHESTRATOR
Adaptive stimulation-side orchestration for brain–spine interfaces · research simulator

A patient's explainer

What each part of the program is, what it does, and how it is meant to help a patient like me walk again.

Concept & author: Ihor Sharanda — patient (Th5–Th6, complete), Lviv, Ukraine · Advisor, First Lviv Territorial Medical Union (1TMO) / Unbroken National Rehabilitation Center · Date: 15 July 2026 · Live demo: brain-spine-orchestrator-production.up.railway.app

In one honest sentence. This is not a medical device and not a finished neuroprosthesis. It is a working, safety-first software “conductor” — a research simulator (a digital twin) that sits between the intent decoder (the brain) and the spinal stimulator (EES). It takes a simple wish to move — “stand”, “take a step”, “climb” — and turns it into the coordinated, precisely-timed stimulation of many muscle groups at once, adjusts it in real time from the body’s feedback, and is physically incapable of leaving the safety limits. I built it as a patient, to understand your field deeply enough to be useful — not to replace the science, but to explore the orchestration that would sit on top of it.
My promise of honesty Every number in this program (currents, timings, angles) is a physiologically plausible placeholder, clearly marked in the code as illustrative and not clinically validated. Where a part is real working software I say so; where it is a stand-in for something only your team and a hardware platform can provide, I say that too. I make no medical claims. I am not asking to be a trial patient. I am simply showing you what I have made, and asking how I might help.

1The problem this program is built around

A spinal cord injury like mine does not damage the brain. My intention to move is intact — the wish to lift my foot is still there, exactly as it always was. What is broken is the cable: the pathway that carries that wish down past the injury to the muscles of the legs. Below the injury, the muscles and the local spinal circuits are often still alive and able to work — they are simply no longer receiving the message.

Your team’s work showed the world that this gap can be bridged — that a decoder can read the intention from the brain, and epidural electrical stimulation (EES) can wake up the spinal circuits below the injury so that walking becomes possible again. That is the miracle. My program does not try to repeat it. It asks a narrower, engineering question:

Once the intention has been read, how do you conduct the stimulation? A single step uses dozens of muscles firing in a precise order, adjusting continuously to balance, fatigue and terrain. Something has to translate “walk” into that living, changing pattern — and never, under any circumstance, deliver an unsafe command. That “something” is what I built: the orchestration layer.

2The whole program at a glance

Think of it as an orchestra. The brain is the composer who decides what music to play. The program is the conductor and the musicians who turn that decision into every note, in time, together — while a safety guardian watches every single note and can silence any one of them instantly. Data flows down (intention → movement) and back up (the body reports what actually happened):

On the pages that follow, each layer is explained on its own: what it is in plain words, what it does, and — most importantly for me — how it is meant to help the patient.

3Every part of the program, explained

Layer 0 — the listener

The Decoder — reading the wish to move

What it is. The entry point. In a real system this is your cortical decoder — the electrodes over the movement area of the brain that read the intention. In my simulator it is a stand-in: the intention comes from a keyboard, a script, or a scenario, so the rest of the program can be built and tested.

What it does. It produces a small, honest signal — Intent — several dozen times a second: what the person wants (stand, step, turn, stairs), how fast, and how confident the reading is. That is all. It never decides to move for the person.

How it helps the patient It keeps the human in command. The movement always begins with the person’s own will — the program only listens. Nothing moves unless you intend it to.
Layer 1 — the conductor

The Orchestrator — turning “walk” into the rhythm of walking

What it is. The heart of the program, and the reason it is called an orchestrator. Walking is not one action — it is a repeating cycle of phases (weight-bearing “stance”, then the leg swinging forward, then both feet down), with the two legs in opposite phases. This layer holds that rhythm.

What it does. It takes the simple wish (“walk”) and, using a model of the body’s own natural gait rhythm — what neuroscience calls a central pattern generator — it breaks the wish into which muscle group should be active, how strongly, and in which slice of the step cycle. It is a conductor keeping time and telling each section of the orchestra when to come in. Crucially, it models walking as a living rhythm, not as a crude “this muscle gets this voltage” lookup — which is both more truthful to how the spinal circuits actually work and what separates a serious concept from a naïve one.

How it helps the patient This is what could give a step its naturalness — a smooth, properly-timed, self-correcting gait instead of stiff, robotic twitching. It is the difference between being moved and truly walking.
Layer 2 — the musicians

The Effectors — one careful specialist per muscle group

What it is. A team of small, focused agents — one for each muscle group and side of the body (hip flexor and extensor, knee, ankle, trunk stabilisers, left and right). Each is a specialist for its own muscle only.

What it does. The conductor says “this muscle, this much, now”. Each effector translates that request into the concrete stimulation settings — how strong, how wide and how frequent the pulses, and through which electrode contacts — using a calibration curve that mirrors how a real muscle responds. It then hands the command onward for safety approval; it can never act on its own.

How it helps the patient Fine, per-muscle control is what makes movement precise and personal. Every body — and every day — is different; this layer is where the stimulation can be tuned to your legs rather than a generic template.
Layer 3 — the sense of balance

Feedback & Sensor Fusion — the program’s eyes and inner ear

What it is. The senses. In a real system these are wearable motion sensors, joint angles, foot-pressure and muscle signals; in the simulator they come from the digital twin of the body. This layer fuses them into one clear picture.

What it does. Many times a second it answers: which phase of the step are we really in? is the body balanced? is something going wrong — a foot dragging, the two sides out of symmetry, balance being lost? It then feeds corrections back to the conductor and the musicians so the next moment is adjusted. This is what “closed-loop” means: the program watches the result and fixes itself continuously, instead of blindly playing a fixed recording.

How it helps the patient This is safety and confidence in motion — the system that catches a stumble before it becomes a fall, and keeps the gait steady on real, uneven ground. It is what lets a person trust their own legs again.
Layer 4 — learning over time

Adaptation — getting better session after session

What it is. The slow, patient learner. It works between steps and between sessions, never in the split-second of a single movement.

What it does. Bodies change — muscles fatigue within a session, respond differently on different days, and settings drift over time. This layer looks at the history of how gait quality is going and gently re-tunes the parameters of the conductor and the musicians: compensating for fatigue, re-calibrating the muscle-response curves, adjusting “for today”.

How it helps the patient It means the system keeps up with you — it does not degrade as you tire or grow stale over the weeks, but adapts, so rehabilitation compounds instead of plateauing. This is exactly where a large, diverse patient cohort would make the learning far stronger.
Layer 5 — the guardian · highest authority

The Safety Supervisor — the one part that can always say “no”

What it is. The most important layer in the whole program, and deliberately the simplest and most predictable. It is plain, fully-tested code with no AI in it at all — because the part that guarantees safety must be transparent and provable.

What it does. Every stimulation command — without a single exception — must pass through it before anything happens. It checks every hard limit (current, pulse width, frequency, charge, a heat proxy, a watchdog for autonomic risk). If a command is safe, it passes. If it is slightly too much, the guardian trims it back to the safe edge. If it is dangerous, it is blocked. And it holds the emergency stop that halts everything at once. No other layer — and no AI — can ever bypass it or overrule it.

How it helps the patient This is the foundation of trust. It is the architectural promise that the technology helping you can never harm you — that no clever component, no mistake, no adaptation gone wrong can ever push past a safe boundary. Safety is not a feature bolted on; it is the layer everything else must obey.
Above the live loop — planning & explaining

The Supervisory AI layer — offline, never in the moment

What it is. A helper that works outside the real-time movement loop, on the timescale of minutes and reports, not milliseconds of a step.

What it does. It translates a clinician’s goals into a protocol configuration, writes plain-language session summaries, and explains why the adaptation made the choices it did. By firm design, AI is allowed only here — for planning and explanation — and is never placed inside the fast motor loop where timing and safety are decided.

How it helps the patient It makes the whole system understandable — to the clinician guiding the therapy and to the patient living it — while keeping the moment-to-moment control in the hands of simple, provable, deterministic code.
The test-bed — a body to practise on

The simulated body (digital twin) & the demo scenarios

What it is. A physics-based stand-in for the legs and the stimulator, so the whole program can be exercised safely without ever touching a person or a device. It receives commands, moves virtual joints, and reports back what happened.

What it does. It runs three complete demonstrations end-to-end — sit-to-stand, walking (including a deliberate push, so you can watch the system correct its balance), and climbing stairs (a change of intent). Each one logs the full chain: intention → gait phases → muscle activations → stimulation commands, before and after the safety check → the body’s response. That full, transparent trace is the proof of concept.

How it helps the patient It lets the whole approach be built, tested and proven safely and honestly first — so that if it ever earns the right to sit on top of a real platform, it arrives already coherent, already safety-checked, already understood.

Where I might genuinely help. The one thing I can offer that a single lab or a single device-maker cannot is clinical scale and patient diversity. An adaptive orchestration layer learns best from exactly that kind of diversity. That is the collaboration I dream of — not to repeat your work, but to help it reach more people.
